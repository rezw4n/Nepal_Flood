## Abstract

On 26 August 2026, a rock–ice avalanche from Langtang Lirung, Nepal, generated a flood that travelled 207 km down the Bhote Koshi–Trishuli valley, leaving {{deaths}} dead and {{missing}} missing. Reconciling five satellite and official damage inventories, we find {{union_damaged}} damaged buildings, {{union_destroyed}} of them destroyed. Height above the pre-event channel dominated a spatially cross-validated model of destruction (AUC {{auc_gbm}}). The height at which destruction became more likely than not fell from about {{h_gorge}} m in the gorge to about {{h_lower}} m 110 km downstream, tracking independently mapped flow margins (Spearman ρ = {{rho_env}}). Built-up area less than 10 m above the channel had grown {{growth_lt10}}-fold since 1975. An automatic seismic alert could have given at least 30 minutes of warning for {{seis_ge30}}% of destroyed buildings, against {{sms_ge30}}% under the SMS actually issued. Damage-calibrated heights offer an objective basis for danger zoning and warning in Himalayan valleys.

**Keywords:** cascading hazard; flood damage; Nepal; Bhote Koshi–Trishuli; height above the river; rapid damage mapping; early warning; recovery planning

## 1. Introduction

Cascading hazards that begin with a slope or glacier failure high in a mountain range and end as a flood far downstream are among the deadliest processes in High Mountain Asia [@Mani2023; @Kirschbaum2019]. Recent examples include the 2021 Chamoli rock–ice avalanche in India [@Shugar2021], the 2021 Melamchi flood [@Chen2024Melamchi; @Maharjan2021ICIMOD] and the 2024 Thame outburst [@Khadka2026NHESS] in Nepal, and the 2023 South Lhonak cascade in Sikkim [@Sattar2025]. Each overwhelmed settlements and hydropower assets that had been built in narrow valley floors, many tens of kilometres from the source. Glacier and permafrost change is altering the likelihood of such failures [@Hock2019; @Ahmed2026; @Bashkova2026], while the population and infrastructure exposed to glacial floods and riverine hazards continue to grow [@Taylor2023; @Zheng2021; @Schwanghart2016; @Li2022NatGeo; @Rentschler2023].

Measuring the damage these floods cause is itself difficult. Research on flood losses has long pointed to incomplete observations, uncertain asset values and the divergence of loss models [@Merz2010; @Gerl2016], problems that are most acute in data-scarce mountain regions [@Romali2025]. Cascading floods add a further complication, because damage develops through the interaction of a moving, sediment-laden flow with the valley it passes through [@Cook2018; @Chen2024Melamchi], so that its pattern can change markedly from one reach to the next.

At 08:37 Nepal Time (NPT) on 26 August 2026, bedrock and part of a hanging glacier failed on the north face of Langtang Lirung at about 5,200 m above sea level [@HiRISK2026; @ICIMOD2026; @Roy2026]. Estimates of the failed volume range from 99 ± 10 × 10^6^ m^3^ [@Roy2026] to about 132 × 10^6^ m^3^ [@ICIMOD2026]. The mass struck the Lhende glacier, briefly dammed the Lhende Khola and released a debris-laden flood that reached the Nepal–China border at Rasuwagadhi within about seven minutes and the confluence with the Kali Gandaki at Devghat, 207 km from the source, in the afternoon [@CHWRR2026; @NDRRMA2026update]. By 3 October, Nepal's National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority (NDRRMA) had recorded {{deaths}} deaths and {{missing}} people missing; Chinese authorities reported {{china_dead}} deaths and {{china_missing}} missing at Gyirong (Kyirong) port [@NDRRMA2026update; @ICIMOD2026]. The government's Rapid Damage and Needs Assessment (RDNA) estimated US${{rdna_damage}} billion of physical damage, US${{rdna_effects}} billion of total effects ({{rdna_gdp}}% of gross domestic product) and US${{rdna_needs}} billion of recovery needs [@RDNA2026]. The consequences extended well beyond buildings: about 10,000 households needed immediate relief, three health posts were fully damaged, one hospital was partly damaged and access to two hospitals was cut, while damaged water and sanitation systems raised further public-health concerns [@WHO2026]. The event followed a supraglacial-lake outburst in July 2025 that had already damaged the same border crossing and hydropower plants [@Xu2026EGUsphere; @ZhangTG2026ACCRE].

Within days, the disaster had been mapped by several independent systems: the Copernicus Emergency Management Service (CEMS) [@EMSR927; @CEMSdash2026], the United Nations Satellite Centre (UNOSAT) [@UNOSAT2026], the Humanitarian OpenStreetMap Team (HOT) [@HOT2026], the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) [@UNDP2026RAPIDA] and the World Bank [@GRADE2026], alongside the government's RDNA. An early analysis of the first rapid maps counted 695 destroyed building points and 15.7 km of destroyed road [@Li2026arXiv]. Such rapid products are indispensable when valleys are cut off [@Voigt2016], but they differ in imagery, coverage, damage scales and purpose, and their reported totals for this event ranged from fewer than 1,000 to more than 12,000 buildings depending on what was counted [@HiRISK2026; @Li2026arXiv; @RDNA2026]. They are rarely reconciled, and even more rarely used to ask why particular buildings were destroyed while neighbours survived. That question now matters for recovery. The government has announced that settlements in high-risk parts of the corridor will be relocated [@RadioNepal2026], but no event-calibrated, physically grounded basis yet exists for deciding where reconstruction is unsafe, and the warning of 26 August reached many riverside communities only after the flood had passed [@ICIMOD2026; @Acharya2026; @XuC2026npj].

This study reconciles the building-damage records of four mapping products and the RDNA into a single corridor-scale inventory, and relates it to the pre-event terrain, to Sentinel-2 surface change, to the documented propagation of the flood wave and to five decades of settlement growth. It asks four questions. How much damage did the products record, and how far do they agree? What controlled which buildings were destroyed? How did the destructive reach of the flood change downstream? And how much warning time was available, and how was it used? We show that height above the pre-event river channel explains most of where buildings were destroyed; that the height reached by destruction declined systematically downstream, in step with independently mapped flow margins and the decay of the wave front; that settlement had expanded fastest into exactly this destructive zone; and that warning time was available for most destroyed buildings but was not used. We translate these results into municipality-level priorities for inspection, debris clearance, danger-zone mapping and early warning.

## 2. Data and Methodology

### 2.1 Study area

The study area is the corridor followed by the flood from the failure on the north face of Langtang Lirung to the confluence of the Trishuli and Kali Gandaki rivers at Devghat (Figure 1; the full corridor is mapped in Section 3.1). Measured along the traced flow path, the flood descended the Lhende Khola to the Nepal–China border at Rasuwagadhi (23 km from the source), followed the Bhote Koshi past Timure to Syaphrubesi (38 km), where the river becomes the Trishuli, and passed Betrawati (69 km), Trishuli Bazar–Bidur (78 km), Galchhi (106 km), Benighat (129 km) and Mugling (170 km) before reaching Devghat ({{path_km}} km). Over this distance the channel falls from about {{z_source}} m to {{z_devghat}} m above sea level. More than {{drop_border}} m of this drop occurs before the border; between Rasuwagadhi and Syaphrubesi the river runs through a narrow gorge in which the valley floor less than 20 m above the river is typically about {{vw_gorge}} m wide, whereas below Betrawati it is about {{vw_valley}} m wide.

The flood affected Rasuwa, Nuwakot, Dhading and Chitwan districts in Bagmati Province and Gorkha and Tanahun districts in Gandaki Province, and the government declared 15 municipalities disaster crisis-affected areas for three months [@WHO2026]. The corridor carries the road to the Rasuwagadhi border crossing, a major trade route between Nepal and China, and a chain of hydropower plants [@HiRISK2026]; 13 hydropower projects with a combined capacity of 759 MW were affected [@RDNA2026]. In 2026 about {{pop_1km}} people lived within 1 km of the river along the corridor, and the September 2026 Overture Maps release contained {{overture_n}} building footprints within 2 km of it [@WorldPopGlobal2; @Overture].

### 2.2 Data

We assembled every openly accessible building-damage record for the event, together with official reports, terrain, imagery, settlement and population data (Table 1). All sources were retrieved between 9 September and 20 October 2026 and archived with retrieval logs and SHA-256 checksums. Damage records comprised (i) the HOT inventory of {{n_hot}} OpenStreetMap footprints graded as no damage, minor, major or destroyed, with an analysed-area polygon [@HOT2026]; (ii) the latest CEMS grading products for the four areas of interest that were graded (AOI01 Syapru Besi, AOI02 Timure, AOI03 Bidur and AOI05 Phosretar), comprising {{cems_damaged}} building points graded as possibly damaged, damaged or destroyed, with area-of-interest polygons, not-analysed areas, imagery metadata and per-area statistics [@EMSR927; @CEMSdash2026]; and (iii) the UNDP RAPIDA building-debris service, which re-published {{undp_total}} footprints attributed to CEMS (2,311), SERTIT (48) and UNOSAT (684) [@UNDP2026RAPIDA]. The UNOSAT flow-extent polygon (37.35 km^2^ within a 363 km^2^ analysed area) was used as an independent observation of the flood footprint [@UNOSAT2026]. Official information came from the NDRRMA situation reports and consolidated update to 3 October 2026 [@NDRRMA2026update], the RDNA [@RDNA2026] and the propagation timeline of the Centre of Hydrology and Water Resources Research [@CHWRR2026].

The building stock was taken from the Overture Maps release 2026-09-23.1 [@Overture], which conflates OpenStreetMap [@Haklay2008], Google Open Buildings [@Sirko2021] and Microsoft footprints; we retained {{overture_n}} footprints within 2 km of the channel. Terrain came from the Copernicus GLO-30 digital elevation model [@CopDEM], settlement history from GHS-BUILT-S R2023A (1975–2020, 100 m) [@GHSBUILTS2023; @Pesaresi2024], population from WorldPop Global2 R2025A constrained counts for 2015, 2020 and 2026 [@WorldPopGlobal2; @Stevens2015], land cover from ESA WorldCover 2021 [@Zanaga2022], infrastructure from Overture transportation and base themes, administrative boundaries from geoBoundaries [@Runfola2020] and imagery from Sentinel-2 Level-2A [@Drusch2012; @EarthSearch].

#### 2.3.1 Harmonizing the damage records

All records were matched to the common building stock. Each UNDP footprint was assigned to the stock polygon with which it shared the largest overlap; each HOT footprint was matched greedily by decreasing overlap, with at most one HOT footprint per stock polygon; and each CEMS point was matched greedily by increasing distance (zero inside a footprint, maximum 10 m) with at most one point per footprint. Records without a match — {{undp_only}} UNDP footprints, {{hot_only}} HOT footprints and {{cems_only}} CEMS points, many of them destroyed buildings deleted from OpenStreetMap after the event — were added to the stock as separate buildings, so that no damage record was lost; the common stock comprised {{stock_n}} buildings. Damage grades were mapped to a common ordinal scale (none; minor or possibly damaged; major or damaged; destroyed), and each building took the most severe grade recorded by any product. Inter-product agreement was measured with Cohen's κ [@Cohen1960] on the {{n_both}} buildings graded by both HOT and CEMS (CEMS AOI01 and AOI02). Because CEMS and UNDP list only damaged buildings, a building was treated as graded-undamaged only if it lay inside a CEMS area of interest and outside its not-analysed areas, or if it carried a HOT label; statistical models were restricted to this graded domain ({{n_domain}} buildings within 1 km of the channel). HOT confidence scores and a 5-m translation of all HOT footprints were used to check the sensitivity of the local labels and of their overlap with the flow extent (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Note 2).

#### 2.3.2 Flow path and height above the river

The four GLO-30 tiles covering 84.3–85.7° E and 27.6–28.5° N were mosaicked and reprojected by bilinear interpolation to a 30-m grid in WGS 84 / UTM zone 45N. The elevation model was hydrologically conditioned with the Priority-Flood+ε algorithm [@Barnes2014], which guarantees that every cell drains to the grid edge, and D8 flow directions [@OCallaghan1984] and flow accumulation were computed. The flow path was traced downstream from the source coordinates reported by HiRISK (28.2887° N, 85.5282° E) [@HiRISK2026] to the Trishuli–Kali Gandaki confluence at Devghat, a distance of {{path_km}} km. The path passed within 0.6 km of every reference settlement and its flow accumulation increased at the confluences of the Budhi Gandaki and Marsyangdi, confirming that it followed the main channel. Channel elevations along the path were made non-increasing downstream by a cumulative minimum, smoothed with an 11-cell running mean and constrained again, which removes artefacts from bridges and vegetation in the surface model.

Height above the channel (relative elevation) was computed for every cell within 6 km of the path as the cell elevation minus the inverse-distance-weighted (power 2) mean of the 16 nearest channel elevations [@Renno2008; @Nobre2011; @GarousiNejad2019]. It measures how far a building stands above the pre-event river, not the depth of the flow. For each building we also computed distance to the channel, distance downstream (chainage), the bank side and whether the building lay on the outer bank of a bend (from the signed curvature of the smoothed path over ±300 m), the local hillslope gradient, the channel gradient over a 2-km window and the width of the valley floor below 20 m relative elevation along a transect perpendicular to the path. The same transects, spaced every 90 m, were intersected with the UNOSAT flow extent to obtain the height of its outer edge on each bank.

#### 2.3.3 Statistical and machine-learning models

For the HOT inventory, the probability of destruction was modelled by logistic regression on height above the channel, and 95% confidence bands and the height at 50% probability were obtained from 1,000 bootstrap resamples of 1-km reaches, which preserves spatial dependence [@Efron1993; @Kunsch1989]. A multivariable logistic model and a proportional-odds ordinal model of the four HOT classes [@Seabold2010] included height (per 10 m), log distance, chainage, valley-floor width, outer-bank position, hillslope gradient, log footprint area and building density, with standard errors clustered by 1-km reach.

For the graded domain, we fitted histogram-based gradient-boosted trees (400 iterations, learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves, minimum 40 samples per leaf, L2 regularization 1) [@Friedman2001; @Pedregosa2011] to predict destruction from nine terrain and building attributes. Performance was estimated by six-fold cross-validation grouped by 5-km reaches, so that test buildings were spatially separated from training buildings [@Roberts2017; @Ploton2020; @Meyer2022], and compared with logistic models using all attributes or height alone. Variable importance was the mean decrease in test-fold AUC over ten permutations [@Fisher2019; @Strobl2007; @Hooker2021]. Two further tests examined dependence on the labels: a model trained and cross-validated on CEMS labels alone within CEMS areas, and a model trained on all labels outside the HOT reach (21.4–41.5 km) and evaluated against HOT's independent labels (Supplementary Note 3).

#### 2.3.4 Damage-calibrated destructive height

The damage-calibrated destructive height was estimated separately for 12 reaches of 5–7 km within the graded areas, as the height at which a logistic model of destruction on height above the channel reached 50% probability, with 500 block-bootstrap resamples of 0.5-km reaches. Where the bootstrap interval exceeded 60 m, we used the smaller of this estimate and the 90th percentile height of destroyed buildings. Reach estimates were smoothed with a three-reach running median within each contiguous graded segment to suppress single-reach outliers caused by a few up-slope records; an outer envelope used the 90th percentile height of destroyed buildings smoothed in the same way. Downstream of the last graded reach, the envelope was interpolated linearly between the stage rises recorded or reconstructed at Galchhi (8.9 m) [@ICIMOD2026], Kali Khola (12.3 m maximum against a normal level of about 3.2 m) and Devghat (6.57 m against about 4 m) [@CHWRR2026]. Agreement with the UNOSAT flow-edge height (median of both banks within each reach) was measured by Spearman's rank correlation (Supplementary Note 4).

### 2.4 Population exposure

GHS-BUILT-S and WorldPop grids were interpolated to the 30-m grid by assigning each fine cell the density of its source cell, which preserves totals. Combining multi-temporal settlement and population layers in this way follows GIS and remote-sensing approaches for tracking how flood exposure changes over time [@Tellman2021; @AhmedR2024Flood]. Built-up surface and population were summed within the UNOSAT flow extent and within bands of height above the channel within 1 km of the path. Two danger zones were then defined from the damage-calibrated heights: a core zone below the reach-wise 50%-destruction height and within 500 m of the channel, and an outer zone below the height that bounded 90% of destroyed buildings. Buildings, surviving buildings and residents in 2026 were counted in each zone. Pre-event land cover inside the flow extent was taken from ESA WorldCover 2021 (10 m) [@Zanaga2022]. Roads, bridges and power assets inside the flow extent were taken from Overture; damaged road lengths and bridge counts graded by CEMS are reported separately (Supplementary Table 13) because they are observations rather than exposure.

### 2.5 Other methods

#### 2.5.1 Sentinel-2 corroboration

Medium-resolution Copernicus imagery can detect building damage in many disasters despite its 10-m resolution, although models trained on past events transfer imperfectly to new ones [@Dietrich2026ISPRS]. We therefore used Sentinel-2 not to grade buildings but to test the labels independently. Clear-sky median composites were built at 20 m from all Sentinel-2 Level-2A scenes with less than 70% cloud cover acquired in October 2025 (pre-event, after the July 2025 flood; {{s2_pre}} scenes) and between 21 September and 18 October 2026 (post-event; {{s2_post}} scenes), keeping only pixels classed as vegetation, bare soil, water or unclassified in the scene classification layer [@Drusch2012; @EarthSearch]. Reflectance was computed with each scene's scale factor; the processing-baseline offset listed in the metadata was applied only where the archive had not already removed it, and reflectance was clipped at 10^−4^ before computing visible brightness, the normalized difference vegetation index, the bare soil index and the normalized difference water index. Where an acquisition had been processed twice, only the later processing was used (Supplementary Note 5). Composites were built per MGRS tile and mosaicked by the larger number of clear observations. Post-minus-pre differences were averaged over a 3 × 3-pixel window around each building, and their ability to separate destroyed from undamaged buildings was measured by the AUC.

#### 2.5.2 Wave timing and warning lead time

Arrival times of the wave front were assembled from the seismic failure time and the CHWRR timeline (Supplementary Table 2) and interpolated linearly along the channel. Gauge-loss times are upper bounds on arrival. Lead time for each destroyed building was the interpolated arrival time minus the alert time under three scenarios: the mass SMS (09:15), the first telephone warnings (08:50, the midpoint of 08:46–08:54) and a counterfactual automatic alert two minutes after the seismic signal (08:39:10) (Supplementary Note 6).

#### 2.5.3 Recovery priorities, debris and economic estimates

Indicators were assigned to local levels using geoBoundaries [@Runfola2020] with spelling harmonized to the RDNA. The composite index combined six indicators: satellite-recorded destroyed buildings, RDNA-affected buildings, government-reported affected people [@UNDP2026RAPIDA], modelled building debris and buildings and population in the core danger zone. In each of 20,000 Monte Carlo draws, weights were sampled from a flat Dirichlet distribution, indicators were normalized by min–max scaling, percentile ranks or logistic-transformed z-scores, and aggregated arithmetically or geometrically; we report the median rank, its 5–95% interval and the probability of a top-three rank [@Saisana2005; @OECD2008; @Tate2012].

Building debris was estimated for the harmonized inventory with the UNDP model: floor area (footprint area × number of floors) × a grade coefficient (1.0, 0.5 and 0.05 t m^−2^ for destroyed, damaged and possibly damaged buildings) × a settlement factor (3, 2 and 1 for urban, peri-urban and rural buildings) [@UNDP2026RAPIDA]. Floors and settlement type were taken from UNDP records where available; otherwise floors were set to the mean for the settlement type and buildings with at least {{urban_thr}} neighbours within 100 m were treated as urban, a threshold calibrated on UNDP's classification. Uncertainty was explored with 5,000 draws in which grade coefficients were scaled by a common factor between 0.6 and 1.4 and the urban factor varied between 1 and 3 (Supplementary Fig. 5). Monetary estimates were not recomputed; we compare published estimates and state their scope.

#### 2.5.4 Reproducibility

All analyses were run in Python 3.14 with NumPy, pandas, Shapely, rasterio, statsmodels [@Seabold2010] and scikit-learn [@Pedregosa2011]. The scripts, a one-command rebuild, retrieval logs and checksums accompany this document. Random seeds were fixed (20260826). No field survey was conducted, no new satellite classifier was trained and no hydraulic simulation was performed.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 The flood and its 207-km impact corridor

The flood wave decelerated sharply as it travelled down the corridor (Figure 3a,b). Using the failure time recorded seismically (08:37:10 NPT) [@Roy2026], the reported arrival at Rasuwagadhi and the times at which river gauges stopped transmitting or crossed warning levels [@CHWRR2026], the front averaged {{cel_1}} m s^−1^ over the first {{ch_rasuwagadhi}} km, {{cel_2}} m s^−1^ to Syaphrubesi and {{cel_3}} m s^−1^ to Betrawati, before slowing to {{cel_trishuli}} m s^−1^ along the Trishuli and {{cel_7}} m s^−1^ above Devghat (Supplementary Table 2). Peak discharge was reconstructed at about 17,000 m^3^ s^−1^ at Galchhi [@ICIMOD2026] and measured at {{devghat_peak}} m^3^ s^−1^ at Devghat [@CHWRR2026]. Damaged buildings were concentrated in two reaches: the confined gorge between Rasuwagadhi and Syaphrubesi (21–41 km from the source), and the wider Trishuli valley between Betrawati and Galchhi (64–114 km) (Figure 3c). The number of people reported dead rose steadily for a month after the event, while the number missing peaked in mid-September before falling to {{missing}} (Figure 3d).

### 3.2 Reconciling the damage record

The products that recorded this damage overlapped only partly (Figure 4a; Table 2). The CEMS photo-interpreted {{cems_assessed}} buildings in four areas of interest using 0.3–1 m imagery acquired between 27 August and 5 September and graded {{cems_damaged}} of them as affected ({{cems_destroyed}} destroyed) [@CEMSdash2026]. The HOT inventory graded {{n_hot}} buildings in the upper corridor with an artificial-intelligence-assisted workflow [@HOT2026], and the UNDP RAPIDA service re-published {{undp_total}} damaged footprints attributed to CEMS, SERTIT and UNOSAT [@UNDP2026RAPIDA]. After one-to-one matching of points and footprints to a common stock of {{stock_n}} buildings (Section 2.3.1), the products together recorded **{{union_damaged}} damaged buildings, of which {{union_destroyed}} were destroyed**; {{union_2plus}} buildings were classed as destroyed by at least two products (Figure 4b). This harmonized total is 2.5 times the 2,359 records available to the first UNDP assessment, because later CEMS monitoring products extended grading to Kispang, Belkotgadhi and Galchhi (Supplementary Table 3).

### 3.3 Height above the river governed destruction

Within the HOT inventory, the share of destroyed buildings fell from {{hot_destroyed_lt20}}% for buildings less than 20 m above the channel to {{hot_destroyed_gt80}}% for buildings more than 80 m above it, while the shares of minor damage and no damage rose (Figure 5c). Height above the pre-event channel — the relative elevation of each building above the traced flow path, similar to height above nearest drainage [@Renno2008; @Nobre2011] — discriminated destroyed from surviving buildings with an AUC of {{auc_rem_local}}; each additional 10 m of height halved the odds of destruction (odds ratio {{or_10m}}). Distance from the channel was a weaker discriminator (AUC {{auc_dist_local}}). In an ordinal model of the four HOT damage classes with errors clustered by 1-km reach, height (odds ratio {{or_rem_ord}} per 10 m), distance, distance downstream and hillslope gradient were all associated with less severe damage, and buildings on the outer banks of bends had 1.5 times the odds of more severe damage (odds ratio {{or_outer}}, 95% confidence interval {{or_outer_ci}}; Supplementary Table 6) — consistent with superelevation and erosion where flows impinge on the outside of bends.

Across the {{n_domain}} buildings in all graded areas, a gradient-boosted classifier [@Friedman2001; @Pedregosa2011] trained on terrain and building attributes predicted destruction with an AUC of {{auc_gbm}} under spatial block cross-validation [@Roberts2017; @Ploton2020] (precision–recall AUC {{prauc_gbm}} at a prevalence of {{prev}}%; Figure 5e and Supplementary Table 7). Height above the channel was by far the most important predictor: permuting it reduced the AUC by {{imp_rem}}, against {{imp_dist}} for distance from the channel and {{imp_chain}} for distance downstream (Figure 5f and Supplementary Fig. 3) [@Fisher2019]. The result did not depend on which product supplied the labels: a model using CEMS labels alone achieved an AUC of {{auc_cems_only}}, and a model trained outside the HOT reach predicted HOT's independent labels with an AUC of {{auc_transfer}}. Height alone retained an AUC of {{auc_rem_only}}, but its effect differed strongly between reaches (Figure 5d), which motivated a reach-specific analysis.

The two zoomed maps in Figure 5 illustrate the contrast. At Timure, in the gorge, buildings were destroyed up to the upper limit of the valley floor, tens of metres above the river. At Trishuli Bazar–Bidur, destroyed buildings were confined to the lower river terraces, below about 20 m, while the town on the higher terrace survived almost intact (Figure 5a,b).

### 3.4 A destructive height that declined downstream

We estimated, for each graded reach, the height above the channel at which the probability of destruction reached 50% (Section 2.3.4). This damage-calibrated height was {{h_gorge_range}} m in the gorge (all gorge buildings pooled: {{rg_gorge}} m), declined from {{h_valley_range}} m between Betrawati and Bidur (pooled: {{rg_valley}} m) and was {{h_lower_range}} m below Bidur (pooled: {{rg_lower}} m) (Figure 6a and Supplementary Table 8). It closely matched an independent measure — the height of the outer edge of the UNOSAT flow extent, mapped from imagery rather than from buildings — across all {{n_reaches}} reaches (Spearman ρ = {{rho_env}}; Figure 6d). Downstream of the last graded reach, stage rises recorded or reconstructed at Galchhi (about 9 m), Kali Khola (about 9 m) and Devghat (about 2.6 m) [@ICIMOD2026; @CHWRR2026] were consistent with the lowest damage-calibrated heights.

The downstream decline in destructive height paralleled the deceleration of the wave front from more than 50 m s^−1^ to less than 10 m s^−1^ and the widening of the valley floor from about 100 m in the gorge to more than 250 m below Betrawati (Figure 6b,c and Supplementary Fig. 4). In the gorge, destruction extended well beyond plausible water depths: only {{gorge_lt20}}% of destroyed buildings lay less than 20 m above the channel, compared with {{valley_lt20}}% in the middle valley and {{lower_lt20}}% in the lower reach (Figure 6e). This pattern implicates the high momentum of a sediment-laden surge confined in a narrow valley, superelevation in bends and the collapse of undercut terraces, rather than inundation alone [@Jakob2012; @PapathomaKohle2017; @Cook2018]. It also means that a single setback distance or flood-mark height cannot define safe ground along the whole corridor (Supplementary Table 9).

### 3.5 Settlement growth into the destructive zone

Built-up surface on the valley floor had expanded far faster than on higher ground (Figure 7a). Within 1 km of the channel in the graded corridor, built-up area less than 10 m above the channel grew {{growth_lt10}}-fold between 1975 and 2020, and that 10–20 m above the channel {{growth_10_20}}-fold, compared with {{growth_ge80}}-fold for land more than 80 m above it (Supplementary Table 10) [@Pesaresi2024; @GHSBUILTS2023]. Inside the area later mapped as flow extent, built-up surface increased from {{fe_1975}} ha to {{fe_2020}} ha, consistent with an independent estimate [@Li2026arXiv]. At Trishuli Bazar–Bidur, much of the riverside building destroyed in 2026 had appeared since 2005 (Figure 7b). The pattern mirrors the faster growth of settlements in flood zones observed worldwide [@Rentschler2023; @Tellman2021] and in Nepal's road and hydropower corridors [@McAdoo2018; @Schwanghart2016]. Before the event, the land later covered by the flow included {{lc_crop}} km^2^ of cropland, {{lc_crop_valley_pct}}% of it in the Trishuli valley, {{lc_built}} km^2^ of built-up land and {{lc_tree}} km^2^ of tree cover (Supplementary Table 15) [@Zanaga2022].

Applying the damage-calibrated height to the whole corridor identifies the ground that the 2026 event showed to be most hazardous. The core zone contained {{dz_core}} buildings, of which {{dz_core_surv}} survived, and an estimated {{dz_core_pop}} residents in 2026 [@WorldPopGlobal2]. It captured {{dz_core_capture}}% of all destroyed buildings. The outer zone contained {{dz_outer}} buildings ({{dz_outer_surv}} surviving) and about {{dz_outer_pop}} residents and captured {{dz_outer_capture}}% of destroyed buildings (Figure 8c). These zones are an evidence base, not a regulatory map, but they indicate the scale of the relocation and reconstruction decisions now facing local and national government [@RadioNepal2026].

### 3.6 Warning time was available for most destroyed buildings

The propagation record shows that warning time was short only in the uppermost reach (Figure 7c). The first official warnings were telephoned from Rasuwa between 08:46 and 08:54, requesting evacuation of residents up to 200 m above the riverbank, and the Department of Hydrology and Meteorology sent {{sms_count}} SMS warnings at 09:15 [@NDRRMA2026update]. By then the wave had passed Syaphrubesi and was approaching Betrawati. Interpolating arrival times along the channel, {{sms_neg}}% of destroyed buildings were reached before the SMS was sent, and only {{sms_ge30}}% received it at least 30 minutes ahead of the flood (Figure 7d). Had an alert been issued automatically two minutes after the seismic signal, which exceeded magnitude 5 [@Roy2026; @USGS2026], no destroyed building would have been reached before the alert, and {{seis_ge30}}% would have had at least 30 minutes (median {{seis_median}} min). Even so, the median warning in Gosaikunda would have been only {{gos_seis}} min, compared with {{bidur_seis}} min in Bidur (Supplementary Table 11).

Mortality cannot yet be located — {{bodies_downstream_pct}}% of the bodies were recovered beyond the corridor, in Chitwan and Nawalparasi [@NDRRMA2026update], and the reported totals were still being revised in late September (Supplementary Table 12) — but the distribution of the missing is consistent with the warning gradient. Rasuwa district, where the flood arrived within minutes of the failure, recorded {{missing_rasuwa}} missing people ({{mpd_rasuwa}} per destroyed building in its local levels), whereas Nuwakot, reached 30–90 minutes after the failure, recorded {{missing_nuwakot}} ({{mpd_nuwakot}} per destroyed building). The difference also reflects the many travellers, pilgrims, customs officers and hydropower workers present at the border on the day of the disaster [@NDRRMA2026update].

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Height above the river as a basis for danger zoning

The 2026 cascade shows that a single, easily measured terrain variable — height above the pre-event channel — explains most of the observed building destruction along a 207-km corridor, and that the height reached by destruction declined systematically downstream. Height above the channel integrates the factors that empirical vulnerability models express through flow depth, velocity and impact pressure [@Fuchs2007; @Jakob2012; @Kang2016; @Prieto2018; @Luo2023], which are not observable for most buildings after an event of this kind. Because the damage-calibrated height agreed closely with the independently mapped flow margins (ρ = {{rho_env}}), with gauged stage rises and with the deceleration of the wave, it captures the physical envelope of the flow rather than artefacts of any one product. The approach needs only a pre-event elevation model, a traced flow path and the damage grading that is now routinely produced within days of a disaster [@Voigt2016].

Three implications follow for recovery in the Bhote Koshi–Trishuli corridor. First, danger zones should be defined by reach-specific heights above the channel rather than by a uniform flood mark or setback distance: a criterion appropriate to the lower Trishuli, where the destructive height was {{h_lower_range}} m, would leave out most of the gorge, where destruction reached about {{h_gorge}} m above the river. Second, {{dz_core_surv}} buildings that survived within the core zone, and {{dz_outer_surv}} within the outer zone, require structural inspection and a decision on whether they can be safely reoccupied; reconstruction in these zones would recreate the exposure that grew fastest before 2026 (Figure 7a). Third, the largest debris and housing burdens lie in Bidur and Gosaikunda, but the uncertain urban debris multiplier and the unexplained RDNA counts below Galchhi should be resolved by field survey before large clearance contracts or compensation are committed. Because buildings recover their function only when the services and networks around them work again [@LiL2023], these decisions also need to be coordinated with the restoration of roads, bridges, water supply and the damaged health facilities [@WHO2026].

### 4.2 Early warning

The warning analysis points to an achievable improvement. Telephone warnings and SMS messages were issued 9 and 38 minutes after the failure but were overtaken by a flood moving at more than 50 m s^−1^ in the upper reach [@ICIMOD2026; @Acharya2026]. Downstream of Betrawati, however, the flood took between 45 minutes and nearly seven hours to arrive. An automated chain that converts a strong seismic signal from a known high-hazard slope into immediate, location-specific alerts — combined with transboundary data sharing, which was absent on 26 August [@XuC2026npj; @HiRISK2026] — would have provided at least 30 minutes for most of the buildings that were destroyed. Whether such warning saves lives also depends on how quickly people can reach safe ground; travel-time models of the kind used to assess access to cyclone shelters in coastal Bangladesh [@AhmedA2024Shelter] could map evacuation times to ground above the damage-calibrated height. The same principle applies to the many Himalayan valleys where hydropower development has drawn settlements and workers onto valley floors below glacial and periglacial sources [@Schwanghart2016; @Li2022NatGeo; @Allen2022NHESS].

### 4.3 Limitations

The analysis has limitations. The 30-m Copernicus elevation model [@CopDEM; @GuthGeoffroy2021] is a surface model built from data acquired before 2015; vegetation, buildings and later changes in the channel, including deposition by the 2025 outburst [@Xu2026EGUsphere], affect heights by several metres, more in narrow gorges. Height above the channel is not water depth: in the gorge it also captures run-up, erosion and terrace collapse. Damage labels were not validated in the field, and mapped damage classes are not equivalent to reference-validated estimates [@Olofsson2014]; the building stock is drawn from open footprints whose completeness varies [@Herfort2023] and some destroyed buildings had been deleted from OpenStreetMap after the event. We therefore restricted inference to graded areas and corroborated labels with independent Sentinel-2 change. Arrival times between gauges were interpolated, and gauge-loss times approximate arrival only to within several minutes. Casualty figures remain provisional and cannot yet be assigned to locations. Finally, the composite ranking reflects the indicators chosen; we report its sensitivity rather than a single order.

## 5. Conclusion

The 26 August 2026 flood killed at least {{deaths}} people in Nepal and left {{missing}} missing as it travelled 207 km from Langtang Lirung to Devghat. By reconciling four rapid-mapping products and the government's damage assessment into a single inventory, and relating it to pre-event terrain, satellite change, the propagation of the flood wave and five decades of settlement growth, this study reached five conclusions.

First, the products together recorded {{union_damaged}} damaged buildings, {{union_destroyed}} of them destroyed. They agreed on destruction but not on lesser damage, and records of unknown provenance, or from areas that no product graded, need checking before they are used for compensation or planning.

Second, height above the pre-event river channel was the dominant control on which buildings were destroyed, far more important than distance from the river or position along the corridor.

Third, the height reached by destruction fell from about {{h_gorge}} m in the gorge to {{h_lower_range}} m below Bidur, closely following the independently mapped flow margin and the slowing of the flood wave. No single setback distance or flood mark can therefore define safe ground along the whole corridor.

Fourth, building on low ground had grown fastest before the disaster: built-up area less than 10 m above the river increased {{growth_lt10}}-fold between 1975 and 2020, and {{dz_core}} buildings, {{dz_core_surv}} of which survived, stand within the core danger zone defined by the 2026 event.

Fifth, warning time was available for most destroyed buildings but was not used. An automatic alert triggered by the seismic signal could have given at least 30 minutes of warning to {{seis_ge30}}% of them, against {{sms_ge30}}% under the SMS actually sent.

For recovery, these results support reach-specific danger zones based on height above the river, structural inspection of surviving buildings inside those zones, field verification of the RDNA counts below Galchhi and of the debris assumptions for Bidur, and an automated, transboundary warning chain for the upper corridor. Because the approach needs only a pre-event elevation model, a traced flow path and the rapid damage maps that are now produced within days of a disaster, it can be repeated quickly after future cascading floods in the Himalaya.
