## Supplementary Notes

### Supplementary Note 1 | Sources, versions and provenance

The analysis combines two kinds of evidence (Supplementary Table 1). Event observations comprise the building-damage grading of HOT, CEMS and UNDP RAPIDA ^1–3^, the UNOSAT flow extent ^4^, the propagation timeline ^5^ and official reports ^6,7^. Pre-event context comprises the Copernicus GLO-30 elevation model ^8^, Sentinel-2 Level-2A imagery ^9,10^, the Overture building stock and infrastructure ^11^, GHS-BUILT-S settlement history ^12^, WorldPop population ^13^ and geoBoundaries local levels ^14^. All sources were retrieved between 9 September and 20 October 2026; every download was logged with its URL, HTTP status, size and SHA-256 checksum.

Three limitations of access shaped the design. Gauge observations of the Department of Hydrology and Meteorology could not be retrieved because the public interface requires an application key, so stage rises were taken from the CHWRR report. HOT, UNDP and UNOSAT layers carry no product-specific redistribution licence; they are cited and archived for verification but should not be republished until permission is confirmed. Planet imagery is released for non-commercial use only and was not used.

### Supplementary Note 2 | Harmonization of damage records and its sensitivity

Grades were mapped to a common ordinal scale: 0, no damage (HOT) or no record inside a graded area; 1, minor (HOT) or possibly damaged (CEMS); 2, major (HOT) or damaged (CEMS, UNDP); and 3, destroyed. Matching to the Overture stock (Methods) left 521 UNDP footprints, 363 HOT footprints and 631 CEMS points without a counterpart; they were retained as separate buildings so that the harmonized total of 5,781 damaged buildings (4,530 destroyed) includes every damage record (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). The 5,781 damaged buildings used in the debris model are the same set.

HOT and CEMS agreed closely on destruction but not on lesser damage (Supplementary Table 4b). Of the 611 buildings that HOT classed as destroyed inside CEMS AOI01 and AOI02, 201 (33%) carried no CEMS record, whereas 97% of CEMS-destroyed buildings were destroyed in HOT; a building recorded as destroyed by either product was therefore treated as destroyed. Only 34% of the 367 UNOSAT-attributed records inside CEMS areas were also destroyed in CEMS, and these records are the only ones available north of the border (Supplementary Table 3).

Two checks retained from the first analysis address the quality of the HOT labels (Supplementary Fig. 6). Requiring a HOT confidence score of at least 0.75 retained 72% of records and removed almost all minor and major labels (minor 155 to 0; major 105 to 4) but only 21 of 677 destroyed labels, so the destruction labels on which the models rest are the most confident ones. Translating all HOT footprints by 5 m in each cardinal direction changed the number inside the UNOSAT extent by at most four buildings and the number of severely damaged buildings inside it by at most four.

### Supplementary Note 3 | Model specification and diagnostics

The local models used the 1,050 HOT buildings with a damage label, of which 677 were destroyed. Height alone separated destroyed from other buildings with an AUC of 0.92 (odds ratio 0.50 per 10 m; height at 50% probability 66 m, 95% interval 57–100 m). In the multivariable models (Supplementary Table 6b), height, distance and hillslope angle are strongly correlated within this narrow reach, so their individual coefficients are less precise than the joint fit. Distance downstream and hillslope angle remained significant in both logistic models and building density in the model of major damage or destruction; in the ordinal model, all terms except valley width and footprint area were significant.

For the regional gradient-boosted model, out-of-fold predictions were well calibrated apart from slight overconfidence at the top of the range (Supplementary Fig. 3a): in the highest decile the mean predicted probability was 0.87 against an observed share of 0.79, and in the next decile 0.59 against 0.60. Partial dependence (Supplementary Fig. 3b–f) shows a sharp decline in the probability of destruction between about 10 and 35 m above the channel and a weaker decline with distance from the channel. Because partial dependence averages over the observed values of all other attributes, including distances of up to 1 km, its 50% contour lies below the reach-wise envelope, which is conditional on the buildings present in each reach; the contour is shown only to illustrate the downstream decline. Permutation importance (Supplementary Table 7b) attributes most of the skill to height above the channel.

### Supplementary Note 4 | Height above the channel and the damage-calibrated height

Height above the channel is a relative elevation: it measures how far a building stands above the pre-event channel, not the depth of the flow. In the gorge it integrates water depth, run-up, superelevation and the collapse of undercut terraces; in the wider valley it approximates the stage reached by the flow more closely. The reach-wise height at 50% destruction is therefore a damage-calibrated envelope rather than a hydraulic quantity.

Reach estimates and their smoothing are listed in Supplementary Table 8. In three gorge reaches (marked †) the 50% height was poorly constrained, with a bootstrap interval wider than 60 m; there the 90th-percentile height of destroyed buildings was used when it was smaller. A three-reach running median within each contiguous graded segment then removed the influence of a few far up-slope destroyed records in the 31–36-km reach, whose 90th-percentile height (146 m) would otherwise have dominated the outer envelope. The pooled estimates for all buildings in each reach group (gorge 62 (95% CI 56–88) m; middle valley 21 (95% CI 19–24) m; lower valley 9 (95% CI 7–11) m) are consistent with the smoothed reach values. The damage-calibrated height correlated with the height of the UNOSAT flow-extent edge across 11 reaches (Spearman ρ = 0.97), negatively with the width of the valley floor and positively with the mean celerity of the wave front (Supplementary Fig. 4). Both relationships share a common downstream trend and are descriptive.

No uniform threshold matched the damage. A 20-m height threshold would have contained 54% of destroyed buildings in the graded corridor but only 16% in the gorge, against 87% in the lower valley, while including 4,232 undamaged buildings along the whole corridor (Supplementary Table 9).

### Supplementary Note 5 | Sentinel-2 processing and corroboration

Sentinel-2 Level-2A scenes were read directly from the Earth Search cloud-optimized archive ^10^. All scenes used had the processing-baseline offset already removed by the archive (property earthsearch:boa_offset_applied), so reflectance was computed as the digital number × 10^−4^; the offset listed in the band metadata was applied only to scenes without this flag. Where an acquisition had been processed twice, only the later processing was used. Medians of clear-sky pixels (scene classes 4–7) were computed per MGRS tile at 20 m for 14 pre-event scenes (2025-10-11 to 2025-10-26) and 19 post-event scenes (2026-09-21 to 2026-10-18), and the tile with more clear observations was kept where tiles overlapped.

All four indices separated destroyed from other buildings (Supplementary Table 5 and Supplementary Fig. 2b). NDVI separated them with an AUC of 0.95 across all graded buildings and 0.94 against HOT labels; the median NDVI change was +0.04 for HOT buildings without damage and −0.33 for destroyed ones. A threshold on NDVI loss chosen without reference to the damage data (Otsu's method ^15^, ΔNDVI < −0.29) delineated 13.1 km^2^ of disturbed ground within 1.5 km of the channel over the first 106 km; 83% of it lay inside the UNOSAT flow extent, which it covered to 56% (intersection over union 0.50). Differences from the UNOSAT extent arise where the flow left vegetation standing, where clouds or terrain shadow removed observations (Supplementary Fig. 2a) and where post-event landslides or construction changed the surface. At 20 m, a building pixel also contains gardens, fields and roads, so the indices corroborate the damage labels at the scale of a building's surroundings rather than grading roofs. The post-event window began on 21 September, a few weeks earlier in the season than the pre-event window, which probably explains the slightly positive median change for undamaged buildings; such a phenological difference cannot produce the grade-dependent contrast observed.

### Supplementary Note 6 | Timing, exposure, infrastructure, debris and ranking

The propagation timeline (Supplementary Table 2) starts at the seismic onset of the failure (08:37:10 NPT) ^16,17^. Gauge-loss times bound arrival from above, and the Kali Khola entry is a threshold crossing that was excluded from the interpolation. Lead times by local level are given in Supplementary Table 11.

Built-up surface grew fastest on the lowest ground in both the graded corridor and the ungraded corridor downstream (Supplementary Table 10). CEMS graded 73.0 km of road as destroyed, 4.8 km as damaged and 14.0 km as possibly damaged in its four areas of interest, and recorded 54 bridges as affected, of which 44 were destroyed (Supplementary Table 13a). 10 road records lacked geometry and were counted but not measured. Overture infrastructure inside the UNOSAT flow extent is listed as exposure in Supplementary Table 13b,c; it records presence before the event, not damage. ESA WorldCover 2021 ^18^ shows what the flow covered (Supplementary Table 15): tree cover, grassland and bare ground dominated above the border and in the gorge, whereas cropland (3.4 km^2^) and built-up land were concentrated in the Trishuli valley. The land-cover areas sum to 37.36 km^2^, matching the area of the UNOSAT polygon (37.35 km^2^).

The debris estimate is most sensitive to the settlement factor applied to urban buildings (Supplementary Fig. 5a,b). The central estimate of 1.04 Mt uses UNDP's factors and therefore lies at the upper end of the Monte Carlo range, whose median is 0.75 Mt; without settlement factors the total is 0.53 Mt. The ranking of local levels was robust at the top and bottom but not in the middle (Supplementary Fig. 5c). In the casualty series (Supplementary Table 12) the number of missing people rose until mid-September and then fell, while the number of deaths continued to rise; the figures used here (to 3 October 2026) remain provisional. Published monetary estimates differ in scope (Supplementary Table 14) ^3,7,19^.
